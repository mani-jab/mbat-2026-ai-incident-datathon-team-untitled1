Generative AI Risks and Safeguards for SEC Victoria

1 Project Setup

This report analyses the AI Incident Database (AIID) to identify recurring generative AI failure patterns and assess what those patterns could mean for SEC Victoria if internal generative AI tools were deployed across retail, wholesale and household-electrification operations.

The analysis follows four questions:

  1. What failure patterns repeatedly appear in generative AI incidents?
  2. How common are those patterns across distinct incidents?
  3. Which of those risks are most relevant to SEC Victoria’s proposed internal uses?
  4. What practical safeguards are supported by the incident evidence?

2 Data Source and Collection Structure

The analysis uses the AI Incident Database collections for reports and incidents.

A report represents a source document describing an AI-related event, while an incident represents the underlying event recorded by AIID. Multiple reports may therefore describe the same incident.

This distinction is important for the SEC Victoria question because counting reports alone could make highly publicised failures appear more common than failures that received less media attention.

6 Defining the Generative AI Analysis Population

The challenge concerns generative AI rather than every form of artificial intelligence represented in AIID.

A reproducible indicator is therefore used to identify incidents whose titles, descriptions or associated reports contain clear generative-AI terminology or major generative-AI system names.

This is a screening method rather than an official AIID taxonomy.

6.1 Build One Text Record per Incident

Code 
incident_analysis_text <- reports_incidents_joined |>
  filter(
    !is.na(incident_id)
  ) |>
  group_by(
    incident_id,
    incident_title,
    incident_description,
    incident_date
  ) |>
  summarise(
    n_linked_reports =
      n_distinct(report_number),

    report_languages =
      str_c(
        sort(
          unique(
            na.omit(language)
          )
        ),
        collapse = ", "
      ),

    report_text =
      str_c(
        unique(
          plain_text[
            !is.na(plain_text) &
              str_trim(plain_text) != ""
          ]
        ),
        collapse = " "
      ),

    .groups = "drop"
  ) |>
  mutate(
    analysis_text =
      str_to_lower(
        str_c(
          coalesce(
            incident_title,
            ""
          ),
          coalesce(
            incident_description,
            ""
          ),
          coalesce(
            report_text,
            ""
          ),
          sep = " "
        )
      )
  )

Working at incident level prevents incidents with many media reports from automatically receiving more weight.

11.1 Recommended Rollout Model

The incident evidence supports a risk-tiered rollout rather than treating every GenAI use case as equally safe.

11.1.1 Tier 1: Low-consequence assistance

Examples include:

  • drafting internal text;
  • summarising non-sensitive documents;
  • brainstorming;
  • formatting content.

These uses can operate with lighter controls, although staff should still verify generated content.

11.1.2 Tier 2: Customer or analytical decision support

Examples include:

  • customer-service assistance;
  • tariff or product explanations;
  • household-electrification guidance;
  • market or commercial analysis.

These uses should require approved information sources, monitoring, clear source attribution where practical and human review before consequential information is relied upon.

11.1.3 Tier 3: High-consequence or operational actions

Examples include:

  • automated customer decisions;
  • actions involving commercially sensitive information;
  • safety-sensitive recommendations;
  • direct interaction with critical operational systems.

The incident patterns support keeping generative AI advisory rather than autonomous in these applications unless the system has undergone substantially stronger validation, governance and assurance.

12 Key Findings

The analysis provides four main findings for SEC Victoria.

First, AI incident reporting is highly uneven. Some incidents receive many more reports than others, meaning raw report counts can exaggerate the apparent importance of highly publicised events. The main risk analysis therefore uses distinct incidents as its unit of analysis.

Second, the identified generative AI incidents contain multiple recurring failure patterns rather than one dominant technical problem. These include incorrect or fabricated outputs, privacy and data issues, unsafe advice, unfair outcomes, misuse and insufficient oversight.

Third, several incidents contain more than one selected failure pattern. This supports a layered-control approach in which verification, human oversight, data governance and security operate together rather than as isolated safeguards.

Finally, the implications differ across SEC Victoria’s operations. Customer-facing and household-support tasks create greater risks from incorrect advice, privacy failures and unequal treatment, while wholesale and internal analytical uses place greater emphasis on verification, commercially sensitive information, security and human approval for consequential decisions.

13 Limitations and Ethical Considerations

The results should be interpreted as evidence about patterns in reported historical incidents, not as a forecast of the probability that SEC Victoria will experience a particular failure.

AIID is an incident database rather than a random sample of all deployed AI systems. Events that receive media, regulatory or public attention may be more likely to appear in the database. Consequently, the prevalence values in this report describe the selected incident corpus rather than population-wide failure rates.

The generative AI screen is based on identifiable terminology and system names. It may miss incidents where a generative model is not clearly described.

The failure-pattern and control-gap classifications are also based on transparent keyword and phrase indicators. They support reproducible pattern analysis, but they should not be interpreted as definitive causal labels.

Reports in multiple languages are retained. However, the current token cleaning, custom stop words and risk dictionaries are mainly English-focused. Multilingual incidents may therefore be classified less completely.

Some incident categories overlap. An incident involving a privacy failure can also involve weak oversight, misuse or security weaknesses. Percentages across categories therefore should not be summed to 100%.

Publication dates identify when source reports were published and may differ from the date on which an incident occurred.

Despite these limitations, the incident-level approach provides a defensible way to use historical evidence to identify controls that SEC Victoria should prioritise before deploying internal generative AI into higher-consequence workflows.

14 Conclusion

The AI Incident Database suggests that the main challenge for SEC Victoria is not simply whether generative AI can produce useful outputs, but whether those outputs can be trusted and governed when they interact with customers, commercially sensitive information and household energy decisions.

The recurring incident patterns support four broad priorities: verify generated information against trusted SEC sources, retain meaningful human oversight, restrict the use of sensitive information, and apply stronger controls as the consequence of an AI-assisted decision increases.

For SEC Victoria, this means generative AI is best introduced through a risk-tiered model. Low-consequence internal assistance can be adopted with relatively light controls, while retail advice, wholesale analysis and household-electrification recommendations should require stronger verification and oversight. Generative AI should not directly control high-consequence operational actions unless substantially stronger assurance has been demonstrated.

## Recommended Rollout Model

The incident evidence supports a **risk-tiered rollout** rather than treating every GenAI use case as equally safe.

### Tier 1: Low-consequence assistance

Examples include:

- drafting internal text;
- summarising non-sensitive documents;
- brainstorming;
- formatting content.

These uses can operate with lighter controls, although staff should still verify generated content.

### Tier 2: Customer or analytical decision support

Examples include:

- customer-service assistance;
- tariff or product explanations;
- household-electrification guidance;
- market or commercial analysis.

These uses should require approved information sources, monitoring, clear source attribution where practical and human review before consequential information is relied upon.

### Tier 3: High-consequence or operational actions

Examples include:

- automated customer decisions;
- actions involving commercially sensitive information;
- safety-sensitive recommendations;
- direct interaction with critical operational systems.

The incident patterns support keeping generative AI **advisory rather than autonomous** in these applications unless the system has undergone substantially stronger validation, governance and assurance.

# Key Findings

The analysis provides four main findings for SEC Victoria.

First, AI incident reporting is highly uneven. Some incidents receive many more reports than others, meaning raw report counts can exaggerate the apparent importance of highly publicised events. The main risk analysis therefore uses distinct incidents as its unit of analysis.

Second, the identified generative AI incidents contain multiple recurring failure patterns rather than one dominant technical problem. These include incorrect or fabricated outputs, privacy and data issues, unsafe advice, unfair outcomes, misuse and insufficient oversight.

Third, several incidents contain more than one selected failure pattern. This supports a layered-control approach in which verification, human oversight, data governance and security operate together rather than as isolated safeguards.

Finally, the implications differ across SEC Victoria's operations. Customer-facing and household-support tasks create greater risks from incorrect advice, privacy failures and unequal treatment, while wholesale and internal analytical uses place greater emphasis on verification, commercially sensitive information, security and human approval for consequential decisions.

# Limitations and Ethical Considerations

The results should be interpreted as evidence about **patterns in reported historical incidents**, not as a forecast of the probability that SEC Victoria will experience a particular failure.

AIID is an incident database rather than a random sample of all deployed AI systems. Events that receive media, regulatory or public attention may be more likely to appear in the database. Consequently, the prevalence values in this report describe the selected incident corpus rather than population-wide failure rates.

The generative AI screen is based on identifiable terminology and system names. It may miss incidents where a generative model is not clearly described.

The failure-pattern and control-gap classifications are also based on transparent keyword and phrase indicators. They support reproducible pattern analysis, but they should not be interpreted as definitive causal labels.

Reports in multiple languages are retained. However, the current token cleaning, custom stop words and risk dictionaries are mainly English-focused. Multilingual incidents may therefore be classified less completely.

Some incident categories overlap. An incident involving a privacy failure can also involve weak oversight, misuse or security weaknesses. Percentages across categories therefore should not be summed to 100%.

Publication dates identify when source reports were published and may differ from the date on which an incident occurred.

Despite these limitations, the incident-level approach provides a defensible way to use historical evidence to identify controls that SEC Victoria should prioritise before deploying internal generative AI into higher-consequence workflows.

# Conclusion

The AI Incident Database suggests that the main challenge for SEC Victoria is not simply whether generative AI can produce useful outputs, but whether those outputs can be trusted and governed when they interact with customers, commercially sensitive information and household energy decisions.

The recurring incident patterns support four broad priorities: verify generated information against trusted SEC sources, retain meaningful human oversight, restrict the use of sensitive information, and apply stronger controls as the consequence of an AI-assisted decision increases.

For SEC Victoria, this means generative AI is best introduced through a risk-tiered model. Low-consequence internal assistance can be adopted with relatively light controls, while retail advice, wholesale analysis and household-electrification recommendations should require stronger verification and oversight. Generative AI should not directly control high-consequence operational actions unless substantially stronger assurance has been demonstrated.
